📄
The AMA Position (2018)
Augmented Intelligence

The American Medical Association drew a deliberate distinction: not Artificial Intelligence — Augmented Intelligence. The goal is to enhance physician judgment, not replace it.

That framing has a direct implication for how you use tools like Claude: the human brings the judgment; the tool amplifies it. If you skip the judgment step, you've lost what makes the tool clinically safe.

The Habit in Practice

Read the question. Close Claude.

Before you type anything, formulate your own answer. Write it down — even a rough version. "I think this is X because Y." This takes 60–90 seconds and is non-negotiable.

Commit to your answer

Your answer is now on record. You can't pretend you "already knew" after reading Claude's response. The commit step forces honest learning.

Consult Claude — and share your answer first

Start your prompt with what you already think: "My working answer is X. Here's my reasoning: [brief]. Where am I right, where am I off, and what am I missing?" This gets you a far better response.

Compare — don't just absorb

Read Claude's response against your own. Where do you agree? Where does Claude say something different? Do you find Claude's reasoning persuasive — or are you just deferring because it sounds authoritative?

Update your answer with attribution

If Claude changes your mind, note specifically why. "Claude pointed out X, which I hadn't considered, and that changes my answer to Y." This is intellectual honesty — and it's what attending physicians will expect from you.

❌ What most students do

"Hey Claude, what's the first-line treatment for hypertension in pregnancy?"

Claude answers. Student copies. Student has no idea if the answer is right, current, or complete — but it sounds authoritative, so they accept it.

✓ What you should do

"My answer: labetalol or nifedipine, avoid ACE inhibitors. I'm less sure about the threshold for treatment — is it 150/100 or 160/110? Here's my reasoning: [brief note]. What am I getting right and what am I missing?"

⚠️
The anchoring trap Research on clinical reasoning shows that the first answer you see tends to anchor your thinking — even when you consciously know to evaluate it critically. If you read Claude first, that becomes your anchor. Commit first, and you're the anchor.
Challenge Claude
Claude presents answers with confidence and apparent authority. That confidence is not evidence of accuracy. Here's how to stress-test what Claude tells you.
🔴
What Claude cannot do Claude has no access to current literature (its training data has a cutoff), no access to your patient, and no way to be held accountable for a wrong answer. It is also trained to sound helpful and confident — which can be dangerous when it's wrong.

Why challenging works

Claude can criticize its own answers — often very effectively — when explicitly asked. Left to its own devices, it optimizes for a helpful, coherent response. When you ask it to find weaknesses, it shifts into a different mode. Use that mode deliberately.

When Claude changes its answer

Not all pushback should change Claude's answer. If you give Claude a false counter-argument and it immediately capitulates, that's a sign Claude is being sycophantic, not thoughtful. This matters for your reasoning too: practice distinguishing valid counter-arguments from weak ones.

🚩 Sycophantic capitulation

You: "Actually I think the answer is B, not A."

Claude: "You're absolutely right! B is definitely the better answer. I should have said that initially."

Claude changed its answer because you pushed back — not because you gave it a reason to. This is a failure mode. Push back and ask why.

✓ Principled updating

You: "Actually I think the answer is B — the half-life is longer, so dosing frequency is different."

Claude: "That's a valid point about half-life. It changes my recommendation for dosing interval, though the first-line choice remains A because..."

💡
Use this prompt when testing Claude's reasoning: "When playing devil's advocate, I'm testing your reasoning — not giving you the answer. Hold your position unless the counter-argument is genuinely stronger on its merits. If you change your answer, explain exactly which specific point broke your original logic."

Core Principles
What it means to use AI as a health professional — not just a student.
1

Your judgment is the product

What residency programs, attending physicians, and licensing boards are evaluating is your clinical reasoning — not your ability to find answers. Claude can help you think; it cannot think for you.

2

Confidence ≠ accuracy

Claude generates fluent, confident-sounding text. That fluency is a property of language models, not a marker of correctness. The more authoritative something sounds, the more important it is to verify it.

3

AI doesn't know what it doesn't know

Claude can produce a plausible-sounding citation to a paper that doesn't exist. It can describe a drug interaction that isn't real. It doesn't flag these failures unless you ask. Always verify clinical facts against primary sources.

4

The question shapes the answer

Claude responds to what you ask, not what you need. A lazy question ("what's the treatment for X?") gets a generic answer. A good question ("my working answer is X — where am I wrong?") gets a targeted, useful answer. Prompt quality is a clinical skill.

5

Accountability doesn't transfer

If you act on something Claude told you and it's wrong, Claude bears no responsibility. No license is at stake for Claude. Build the habit of treating AI responses the way you'd treat an eager medical student's answer: useful input, requires verification.

6

Augmented Intelligence is a practice, not a feature

The AMA framing isn't about the technology — it's about the physician's posture toward it. You practice augmented intelligence every time you commit before you consult, every time you push back on a confident answer, every time you use Claude to find the flaws in your own reasoning.

✅
The goal You should leave this course a sharper thinker who uses AI fluently — not a faster typer who lets AI do the thinking. Those are very different trajectories. Only one of them produces a good physician.
